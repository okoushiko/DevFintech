Mission

Nobody teaches you this in school.

Most students graduate knowing how to solve for x and nothing about how a credit score works, why they overspend, or what a bank actually does with their money. We fix that with free classes taught by people their own age.

Our programs cover the fundamentals of finance, the behavior behind every money decision, and the technology reshaping both. Every dollar we raise goes toward putting laptops in the hands of students who don't have one, because a class you can't log into isn't free.

Course catalog

Everything a chapter can teach.

Each course comes with a slide deck, a lesson plan, and at least one activity that gets students working with real numbers instead of watching someone else do it.

01

Personal Finance

Budgeting, saving, and managing money day to day. Students build a budget from a real income figure, track where it leaks, and set up a savings rule they can actually keep. This is the course most chapters open with, because everything else assumes it.

FinanceStart hereAll ages
02

Behavioral Finance

The psychology behind spending, investing, and money decisions in general. Loss aversion, anchoring, mental accounting, and the sunk cost trap, taught through the choices students are already making. The point isn't to name the biases, it's to catch yourself in one.

BehavioralDiscussion-heavy
03

Banking

What a bank actually does with your deposit, and why that matters to you. Covers account types, how checking and savings differ, what fees are hiding where, and how to manage income and expenses through an account instead of a shoebox.

FinancePractical
04

Investing

An introduction to the ways money grows: stocks, index funds, bonds, and the compounding math that makes starting early matter more than picking well. Students leave able to read a ticker, explain diversification, and spot a promise that's too clean to be true.

FinanceMath-light
05

Coding

Python and HTML from nothing. Variables, loops, and functions on the Python side, structure and styling on the web side, aimed at a small finished project rather than a certificate. Finishing this course is what gets students ready for our workshops and hackathons.

FintechNo experienceProject-based
06

Economics

Supply and demand, inflation, interest rates, and why the price of everything moved last year. We offer this course to students 11 and older so the abstractions land instead of washing over the room.

FinanceAges 11+
07

Digital Currencies

Cryptocurrencies, the basics of blockchain, and how to keep your financial information safe online. Equal time goes to the technology and to the scams built on top of it, because the second one is what students meet first.

FintechSafety
08

Loans

An overview of loan types, what each one is used for, and the terms that come attached. Student loans, auto loans, mortgages, and credit cards, read side by side, so an APR stops being a number nobody checks.

FinanceBefore college

Debt

The different kinds of debt, how they compound, and how to manage and reduce them. Students compare payoff strategies on real balances and see what an extra payment a month actually does to a timeline.

In practice

What a semester looks like.

Nothing here is rigid. This is the shape most chapters land on after a term or two, and it's the default we hand new leads so they aren't designing from scratch.

WEEKS 1–3

Ground it

Personal Finance. Everyone builds a budget in week two, then compares them. It's the session that convinces members this club is worth showing up to.

WEEKS 4–6

Explain the pull

Behavioral Finance. Why the budget from week two already broke. Discussion-led, so it works even in a room that doesn't like lectures.

WEEKS 7–10

Open the machine

Banking, Loans, and Investing. The mechanics of where money sits and what it costs to move, taught with real statements and real rates.

WEEKS 11–14

Build something

Coding and Digital Currencies, ending with a small project. Chapters often time this to land right before a hackathon.

Outcomes

What a member leaves with.

Not a certificate. A set of things they can do on a Tuesday afternoon without asking a parent for help.

  • A budget that survives contact. Built around their own spending, not a template, and revised once they've seen where it breaks.
  • The ability to read the fine print. APR, minimum payment, compounding period, and what each one costs over a real timeline.
  • A working project. Small, theirs, and running, which is a very different feeling from having watched a tutorial.
  • A scam radar. The specific shapes financial fraud takes online, and the habit of checking before clicking.

The path

Idea to a room full of students.

Three steps, and the longest one is waiting on your school's club approval process, which is out of our hands and usually takes a couple of weeks.

01

Apply to lead a chapter

Tell us your school, your grade, and why you want to teach this. We read applications on a rolling basis and reply within about a week. You don't need a finished plan, a co-lead, or a faculty advisor lined up yet.

02

Get the material and training

Slide decks, lesson plans, and activities for all nine courses, plus a training call where we walk through the first three sessions with you. You'll teach one back to us before you teach it to a room.

03

Run your club

You set the meeting time and the pace. We stay on call, pass along what's working at other chapters, and pull your members into hackathons and national events.

The deal

What you get, and what we ask.

What we hand you

  • Nine full course kits. Slides, lesson plans, activities, and answer keys, ready to teach without prep beyond reading them.
  • Training before your first class. A call, a practice session, and notes on the parts students reliably get stuck on.
  • Branding and a club page. Logo files, a poster template, and a signup form so recruiting week isn't a design project.
  • The network. A shared channel with the other chapter leads, where a fix that works at one school reaches the other fifty-nine.
  • Event access. Your members get into Compound Hacks and our workshops, and you get a say in what we run next.

What we ask of a lead

  • A real meeting cadence. Weekly or every other week. A club that meets twice a semester isn't teaching anyone anything.
  • Roughly two hours a week. One to run the session, one to prep it. Less once you've taught a course before.
  • Keep it free. No dues, no paywalled material, no upselling anything to your members. Ever.
  • A short check-in each term. How many students showed up, what landed, what didn't. It's how the material improves.
  • Hand it off. Before you graduate, train whoever takes over. Chapters that skip this step don't survive the year.
Roles

You don't have to run the whole thing.

Most chapters split the work across three or four students. If you'd rather teach one course than lead a club, that's a real option, say so on the form.

Owns the schedule, the room, and the relationship with the school. Runs the term check-in and picks who takes over next year.

Educator

FAQ

Questions we get a lot.

Is it really free?

Yes. Classes, materials, and events cost students nothing. We're a nonprofit, so donations and sponsorships cover the running costs, and the rest buys laptops for students who need one.

Who can join a chapter?

Any student. Chapters are usually school clubs, so start by checking whether your school already has one. If it doesn't, you can start it.

My school doesn't have a chapter. Can I start one?

That's how most of our 60+ chapters began. Fill out the get-involved form with your school and grade, and we'll send you the application and the material.

Do I need a faculty advisor first?

No. Apply first, and we'll give you something concrete to show a teacher, which makes that conversation much easier than describing an idea. Most schools require an advisor to charter a club, so you'll need one eventually.

Do I need to know how to code?

No. The coding course starts from zero, and the finance and behavioral courses don't touch code at all. Plenty of members never write a line and still get everything out of it.

We already have a finance or business club. Can you just come teach?

Yes, and it's a common setup. Pick that option on the form and we'll plan sessions around your existing meeting schedule instead of starting a second club that competes with yours.

How much time does leading a chapter take?

Around two hours a week: one to run the session, one to prepare it. It drops after your first term, once you've taught a course before and know where the room slows down.

Where does the money go?

Into electronics for students who can't afford them, and into running free events. Nobody at DevFintech is paid, every instructor and organizer is a volunteer.

Can adults or organizations help?

Yes. Mentors, guest speakers, sponsors, and in-kind donors all make a real difference. Email thedevfintech@gmail.com and tell us what you'd like to do.

How we started

Nobody taught us this either.

DevFintech began the same way most of our chapters do now: a small group of students who noticed nobody in their school actually understood money, and decided to fix that themselves instead of waiting for a class that was never going to appear on the schedule.

What started as one club has grown into a nonprofit with 60+ chapters and more than 1,500 students taught, all still run the same way, students teaching students, for free.

We're not waiting for financial literacy to show up in the curriculum. We're just teaching it.
